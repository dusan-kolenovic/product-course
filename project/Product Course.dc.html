In times of AI, the role of the Product manager remains the same: build solutions which people love, which seamlessly fit into their lives and help solve their business problems.

We will talk about what's actually different now: the products, how they behave, the work you hand over to AI, and how you choose, launch and measure what you build.

01 Welcome

Product Management is a wonderful job — and predominantly a people business.

It carries your personality with everything you do. The four most important skills of a Product Manager:

1

Curiosity

You act as air cover for people around you, connecting them and initiatives to build cumulative product value. Asking good questions is your best move.

The true state of your product doesn't live in any single document. The best source of truth is an internal feeling you build over time by triangulating Jira reports, watercooler conversations, macro trends and board discussions.

2

Communication

People are coming to you with ideas. It is your job to make sure only the right ones deserve their spot on production. You are the ultimate leader without formal authority.

Product is a connective function, so communication is most of your job. Without formal authority, your influence runs on trust — so build the right relationships while everything is going great.

Did you know you are multilingual? You speak business value, technical feasibility, sales opportunity and marketing approach as you navigate different stakeholders and use their language.

To every conversation

Bring choices, never ultimatums. Provide options and modularity, and present clear trade-offs ("If we move this up, what do we push out?"). Where a stakeholder feels like they are losing something, the least you can do is give them optionality and a sense of control.

Share bad news early, with options: what could happen, how likely, how far it reaches, who owns it and what you propose. Avoid watermelon reports — all green outside, red inside.

When something breaks, start curious: "Glad we found this now rather than later. Tell me more." Then check what it affects downstream: scope, deadline, budget, quality.

Managing up

Leadership cares about revenue, growth and predictable results. Instead of a list of features, build a narrative around numbers and a forecast; speed, quality, cost. The more senior the room, the fewer details outside of this are needed.

3

Authenticity

In who you are, and what you build. The best products aren't for everyone — but the people who use them, love them.

4

Comfort with ambiguity

Value doesn't have to wait for perfect conditions. Your intuition holds value.

Principles
1

Narrow scope always beats wide scope, and end-to-end delivery beats loose ends. Deliver simple, end-to-end usable increments first — and only then add more features.

+ Napoleon's passion for immediate usefulness

Napoleon "would make sure that it was built in stages and that at the end of each stage it could be put to some use... If the wing of a palace, or a stretch of road or canal, could be put to use after the first year, then its cost began to be amortized. By the time the whole was completed, it had paid for itself."

2

Development tools are available to more people than ever. Your job is to add sensible friction and steer this potential towards maximal value — which takes curiosity and a holistic view of the business.

3

You (and your products) don't do everything for everyone.

4

Every line of code is an investment. Features are constantly competing to earn their place on the Roadmap (and Production). You hold this logic by tracking their potential and current contribution to business goals.

+ learn more: TARS

TARS (from Reforge) checks whether a feature has earned its spot on production: who it's for (Target audience), how many of them tried it (Adoption), how many kept using it (Retention) and how many of those are happy (Satisfaction). Each step is a share of the one before, so you can see exactly where value leaks.

Example: recommendations in an online shop target 30% of active users. 20% of them adopt it in month one, 40% of those are still using it after three months, and 80% of those are satisfied.

5

You can truly change only what you actively measure. Don't be afraid to define "common knowledge": what is a good experience? How does success actually look? How do we know we've reached Product-Market-Fit?

6

The quality of the outcome is reflected in the quality of the process. At least half of your job lives in "soft" space — changing how the organization works, negotiating priorities, connecting stakeholders.

AI products are still products. They exist to solve problems.

The core principles stay the same. Everything outside of them has changed — and that is the topic of this course.

02 New forms of products

A product is something which solves a relevant problem. Hold that, and you'll move easily through different product shapes.

Shape one

API as a product

The product is a capability that other people's code uses, so your user is a developer. Success means a developer can get value fast and on their own.

Stripe

Stripe sells payments as a few lines of code. Its developer docs and quick setup are the product experience — that's why so many companies built on it and never left.

Shape two

Data as a service

Think about which data your solutions touch — and how, separate from your current solution, it would bring value for the business.

The product is the data itself: clean, trusted and kept up to date, delivered on demand. You don't compete on features — you compete on accuracy, coverage and freshness.

Nielsen

Nielsen doesn't make a single TV show. It just tells the industry who watched — and billions in advertising are priced on that number.

Shape three

Productized service

Custom expert work turned into a repeatable offering with a fixed scope and price — often packaged as software with tools.

Databricks

Building data and AI pipelines used to mean hiring specialists for every project. Databricks packaged that expertise into a ready-to-use platform, so customers get expert-level results without hiring the experts.

03 New nature of AI products

From deterministic to probabilistic.

The new ways we build and use AI products as Product Managers.

Loops & layers

Projects run in cycles. Self-healing products run in loops and layers, where each layer's output is the next one's input:

1 · Sense

What users do, who they are, what's changing in the macro space.

2 · Synthesise

Signals are cross-referenced against business goals; a fresh tech-business hypothesis is printed out.

3 · Ship

The smallest slice that moves the KPI is built by agents, with a human in the loop.

4 · Validate

Synthetic audience, previews and release rings make sure the rollout scales as KPIs are hit.

With each loop the product becomes smarter; iterations get cheaper and faster as you move towards a self-healing product.

Speed

No one is holding their breath for large release cycles — they get replaced by a shorter cadence of slice deployments.

Ship → Measure → Repeat

You'll know you're at the right pace when your release notes start looking like a chat thread.

Spotify

Spotify now ships about 4,500 production deployments a day. Idea to production used to take weeks or months — now it takes about an hour.

Building ahead of time is normal

Classical roadmaps commit only to what today's technology can reliably deliver. AI products deliberately ship a capability before the model is fully ready to support it. For the first time in history, you are literally building for tomorrow.

Anthropic

Built the harness for code review before the model was good enough. It shipped the moment accuracy crossed the team's trust threshold — and a shelf of such prototypes is re-tested with every model release.

Compounding products

Traditional features ship once and get used many times. The AI way makes solutions smarter (and cheaper) over time, and extends them across personas and industries seamlessly.

Dynamic products

Traditional tools stay roughly as capable as the day they shipped. An AI solution decays without active ownership of its guardrails and eval context.

Cleaning is part of building

Every model release is a signal to remove something. Prompts, guardrails and workarounds built for older models add unnecessary complexity — revisit them. Anthropic cut 20% of a system prompt in one upgrade.

Teams now own problems

The classical team is organized by function — product, design, engineering, quality. The AI-native team reorganizes around the problem it needs to solve.

One caution: fast deployment in slices can leave the customer experience fragmented, breaking the user's mental model. Continuous usability testing and a great partnership with the QA crew keep it feeling like one product.

DoorDash

Small teams of 3–5 people, each owning a domain end-to-end, move 3–5x faster with AI.

Completely new adoption frictions

Identity threat

Expert users resist AI tools that imply "the machine knows better." Design AI to support their judgment, not replace it.

Automation bias

Users who trust the AI too much stop checking it. Be transparent with confidence levels and sources, and send uncertain cases to a human.

Accountability gaps

AI solutions are still tools. If no one knows who owns an AI-assisted decision, adoption drops. Put a named person behind every decision.

Your role is changing

The execution part of your job is getting automated: backlog grooming, PRD drafting, ticket writing, status updates. What stays is judgment, taste and vision — choosing the right problems, defining what "good" looks like, looking ahead on the business and model capability, designing the guardrails and eval sets.

04 Uniquely human

Judgment. Taste. Vision.

Models are trained on what was already done. They have no access to what is felt, or the shared experience behind genuine connection. When every operational aspect of your work gets automated, your judgment, vision and taste are left — your authentic human perspective on work.

How do I know what to hand over to AI?

Three flavours to choose from — the cheat sheet:

AutomationAugmentationAgentic

Automation

AI takes over the entire process, zero supervision.

The process is repetitive, has clear steps you can write in advance, is tedious, not creative, and always has the same goal. Every run takes the same path, "good" output can be clearly measured, and errors are cheap and easy to spot.

Examples

Pulling name, date and total from an invoice · turning a list of specs into a product description · localising documentation while keeping its tone.

Augmentation

AI handles the volume, the human handles the judgment.

High volume and pattern-rich, but the stakes are high, so accountability stays with the expert, who verifies quality, viability and compliance. "Good" is judged by eye — no objective pass or fail — so AI acts as a sparring partner while the human sets the direction. Augmentation moves the human from creator to curator.

Examples

AI drafts the requirements and design spec from the PM's intent; the product owner reviews instead of writing · AI builds three throwaway prototypes of a new screen; the team clicks through and picks a direction by eye.

Agentic solutions

AI acts on its own toward a goal, deciding the next step at runtime.

The goal is clear, but the path isn't. Work has to be broken into subtasks and progress evaluated along the way, not at the end. It spans many sources and formats and needs memory to do its work.
i
A tell-tale sign you need this: your automated workflow needed many exceptions.
Examples

Monitoring an industry news feed and alerting legal only when a regulatory change affects current products · extracting liability clauses from 50 contracts and flagging deviations from the standard.

Of course, this is a spectrum. The same workflow moves from augmentation toward automation as trust and specification improve — and with each model release the lines move. Revisit your decisions here.

Check your surroundings. Are you striking the right balance between your (human) work and AI?

05 What to build?
WHAT you decide to build is as equally important as HOW you decide. The quality of an outcome is reflected in the quality of the process behind it.

Production reality

Production reality exists long before your solution does. It is how the work is done today: the real people, the real workflows, the real data, the tools they already juggle and the workarounds they built to get through the day.

Your product will not land in empty space. It lands inside a rich context of actual workflows and the psychological expectations of your stakeholders. Understanding both marks the difference between your audience accepting or rejecting your solution.

Ask yourself

How informed are you about the workflows and expectations of your audience? What is done "because we've always done it this way" — and could clearly be improved? Let me know.

i
The cheapest way to resolve a debate isn't a meeting — it's a prototype and data. Evidence forms conviction better than endless argumentation.

Roadmap

A roadmap is a hypothesised sequence of solutions at a given moment, estimated to bring the biggest ROI in the shortest amount of time. It is a snapshot in time, a dynamic artefact — and you own it.

There is one unified product roadmap — no person X and person Y roadmap, no separate tech-debt roadmap. It can carry infrastructure, features, design and process work. Not all of it touches users, but all of it creates value.

"Cut onboarding to one day" is a roadmap item; "build an onboarding experience" is a task.

Shorter horizon

Anthropic's Claude Code six-month roadmap held up for about three months. They didn't respond with better forecasting — they simply started planning in shorter intervals.

Shape 1
Feature roadmap

What gets built and roughly when. Still right for regulatory deadlines, mature solutions and low-risk work on known tech.

Shape 2
Outcome roadmap

Each team gets a business objective and decides how to reach it. Much more adjusted to the AI space. Cagan's example: "dramatically reduce time for a new customer to go live" — success is onboarding under 3 hours.

Shape 3
Strategic intent

For leadership: no features, no dates. Where we want to be, why it matters, which constraints guide us. The team keeps the freedom to choose the path.

The new risk

You want to avoid deploying 100 individually successful slices which, in totality, break your product experience. Partner with User Insights and QA: continual usability testing catches whether a new slice introduces friction, breaks a mental model, or fragments the experience across cohorts.

Synthetic audience

An AI model of your customers which you can access at any time. It is the cheapest gate in your validation loop — used to exclude bad ideas early, before a real user spends time on them.

Feed it, don't just buy it. Accuracy depends on how much real data flows in: a panel connected to your interviews, surveys, support tickets and behavioural data becomes a living audience that gets sharper with every loop.

Pro tip: always pair it with real people. Synthetic and human forecasts combined beat either one alone — treat it as augmentation, not a replacement.

From the field

Once live behavioural data was piped into an Electric Twin panel, usability recordings arrived pre-coded by theme — and insight-to-action dropped from two weeks to two days.

06 Roll-out & GTM

Your product can change after launch — without anyone touching a line of code.

As models get updated and customers bring inputs you never tested on. A rollout is a continuous process of releasing your product, in which each stage is unlocked via measured criteria.

1 · Written conditions for roll-out

Each functionality competes for its spot on production. Have in written form what it needs to achieve in each phase to progress to the next one:

0
Shadow mode

The AI runs on real production cases next to the current process; no end-user sees its output.

Unlocks when the AI matches the current process on 99% of cases.
1
Friendly customers

3–5 existing end users who agree to try a beta and give feedback in a structured manner you set up.

Unlocks when users naturally switch over without being asked, and no critical issue is open.
2
One segment

5% of accounts in one group of similar customers — segmented by usage type, pricing tier, geography.

Unlocks when task success is at least 5% higher than without your solution, and cost per decision is within budget.
3
All segments

25% of accounts across every user group.

Unlocks when the same lift holds in each group individually, not only on average.
4
Everyone

All accounts. The old path is switched off and everyone uses the new solution.

If a solution misses its condition in its dedicated time, stop the progression and iterate — or roll back with a feature flag, depending on severity. Share these conditions with stakeholders before the rollout, so promotions and rollbacks are clear.

2 · Map the mental models waiting in production

Your product lands on expectations customers already hold. Before launch, list the ones it will meet on day one and plan around them.

Anthropic reports ~1/3 first-attempt success for its coding agent, so it sets the expectation up front: try one-shot, then switch to collaborating. Notion saw 18,000 agents created in three weeks — 90% running on automated triggers, not chat. Usage data, not the launch plan, showed how the product was adopted.

3 · Let the cost of an error set the pace

Cheap and visible (a draft the user edits before sending): take bigger steps — 5% → 50% of accounts, about a week per phase.

Costly or hidden: start in parallel with the existing process, then 1% → 5% → 20%, about a month per phase, with human approval on every action. And compare against the old process's measured error rate — it makes mistakes too.

4 · Design adoption, then measure it per segment

Put the entry point inside the workflow where the work actually happens — not in a separate AI tab. Usage unlocks usage: track actions as kinetic energy which spreads and drives more usage.

Facebook famously found that a new user adding 7 friends in 10 days hit the "Aha! moment" — beyond it, long-term retention skyrocketed; below it, they almost always churned.

5 · Measure the outcome your customer bought

Logins show the product is open, not that it works. Pick the one number that proves the customer got the result — a resolved ticket, a completed task — and report it next to usage.

Intercom prices its Fin agent at $0.99 per confirmed resolution — the outcome metric doubles as the invoice.

Pricing

The biggest marginal lever for growth is not acquiring new customers — it's pricing.

Price is an exchange rate between the value your customer receives (and perceives) and what they pay for it. Get close to understanding how your end users interpret value. The gap between what customers would pay and what you charge is your revenue left on the table.

How to know what to price

A simple grid shows where each functionality lies — and from that, how to position and price it. Different segments see value differently; use that to maximize consumer surplus. Features only some customers want (a lot) work best as add-ons.

The classic add-on

Priority support: 20–30% of a customer base will pay for it. Price it around 10% of the core product — since you already support your customers, it's almost all pure margin.

Pricing is a continual conversation

Review pricing every 6 months as the product improves; run pricing experiments every quarter. The logic is simple: once you've delivered new value, you can raise the price. When you re-price, back it with research and protect existing customers (grandfathering), so it reads as value and not as a grab.

Pricing in times of AI

Outside of classic infra cost, AI products produce inference cost just by being used. The old freemium — unlimited access — has to change: free has to show enough "magic" to hit the aha moment, without being good enough to replace the paid tier. The question on your table: what to gate behind the paywall?

Google AI started with one premium tier built around its smartest model. It didn't work: the free tier already felt "smarter than I am" to most users, so few upgraded — while paying power users burned "terrifying" amounts of compute.

What Google gates instead

Usage intensity — tiers by volume (Midjourney: same quality, you pay for fast GPU hours).

Outcomes — free gives the answer, paid does the work (Chrome auto-browse).

The heaviest modalities — text free; video, 3D and simulation paid.

Monetization

You have a new cost line item: the price of every decision your AI makes.

Each decision is paid in model calls, tokens, retrieval and tool runs — track it per decision, not only per customer. Pricing sets the number; monetization is the mechanics behind it: how the bill is built, how it behaves month to month, and whether each customer still earns you money.

Cost to serve sets the floor

Your cost per decision sets the floor of your price — and that floor moves with every model you choose and every new release. On the Databricks calculator, the same workload costs $3,823 a month on GPT-5 and $152 on GPT-5 Nano. Model choice is now a monetization decision.

Predictability is a feature

When a bill surprises, customers don't just question the price — they stop trusting the product. Bill shock is a product problem, not a billing one. The fix: a committed base agreed upfront, overage priced in advance, and real-time usage visibility so alerts arrive before the spend.

Lebara turned bill shock into a feature: a usage alert at 80% and a one-tap top-up, instead of a support ticket.

AI keeps the bill under control

The same AI that adds a cost line helps you manage it: forecast usage per cohort and flag anomalies before the invoice goes out; answer "why did my bill change?" with the actual root cause; replay a planned price change on real customer bills before you ship it.

Try this

Pick your heaviest and your lightest customer. Compare what each pays, the value each gets, and what each costs you to serve — inference included. If your heaviest user is your least profitable one, your model charges for the wrong thing.

07 What success looks like

A question you should be the first to propose a response to.

Then: how do you measure it, with which tools, and who gets the data? That's the Metrics strategy you own. In times of AI it has moved from periodic snapshots to continuous user intelligence — a quarterly survey becomes an always-live loop, with a synthetic audience that compresses concept tests from weeks to days.

Quality & Trust

  • Eval coverage — % of real production scenarios in your golden set
  • Eval pass rate as a merge / release gate
  • Hallucination ratio
  • Regeneration rate — how often a user hits "retry"
  • Guardrail trigger / policy-violation hit rate
  • Trace coverage — % of decisions with an auditable log
  • Re-engagement after error rate

Autonomy & Reliability

  • Autonomy rate based on task complexity
  • Types of tasks automated (by complexity)
  • Ratio of AI decisions overridden by a human
  • Model drift frequency

Value & Adoption

  • Depth of use and organic (unprompted) adoption
  • Goal completion rate
  • Time-to-data / time-to-value
  • Flywheel coverage — quality outputs fed back to the system
  • Synthetic-audience accumulation rate

Unit Economics

  • Inference cost-per-decision, weighed against the value each call creates
  • Cache hit rate
