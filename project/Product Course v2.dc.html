Principles
1

Narrow scope always beats wide scope, and end-to-end delivery beats loose ends. You want to deliver simple, end-to-end usable increments first. And only then add more features.

Napoleon "would make sure that it was built in stages and that at the end of each stage it could be put to some use, no matter how many years might elapse before the whole was completed. If the wing of a palace, or a stretch of road or canal, could be put to use after the first year, then its cost began to be amortized... By the time the whole was completed, it had paid for itself."
2

In a world where development tools are available to more people than ever, your job is to add sensible friction and steer this potential towards maximal value. For this you need curiosity and a holistic approach to the business.

3

You (and your Products) don't do everything for everyone.

4

Every line of code is an investment. Features are constantly competing to earn their place on the Roadmap (and Production). You hold this logic by tracking their potential and current contribution to business goals.

TARS (from Reforge) checks whether a feature has earned its spot on production: who it's for (Target audience), how many of them tried it (Adoption), how many kept using it (Retention) and how many of those are happy (Satisfaction). Each step is a share of the one before, so you can see exactly where value leaks.

Low adoption points to discovery or onboarding. Low retention means the feature doesn't deliver lasting value, so iterate on it or sunset it.

02New forms of products

If you consider a Product as something which solves a relevant problem, you won't have any issues moving through different product shapes.

After this chapter, you can answer{{ qc.c2 }}
Shape one

API as a product

The product is a capability that other people's code uses, so your user is a developer. Success means a developer can get value fast and on their own.

Stripe

Stripe sells payments as a few lines of code. Its developer docs and quick setup are the product experience, and that's why so many companies built on it and never left.

Shape two

Data as a service

Think about which data your solutions are touching and how, separate from your current solution, they would bring value for the business.

The product is the data itself: clean, trusted and kept up to date, delivered on demand so customers can use it in their own decisions and systems. You don't compete on features. You compete on accuracy, coverage and freshness.

Nielsen

Nielsen doesn't make a single TV show. It just tells the industry who watched, and billions in advertising are priced on that number.

Shape three

Productized service

This turns custom expert work into a repeatable offering with a fixed scope and price, often packaged as software with tools.

Databricks

Building data and AI pipelines used to mean hiring a team of specialists for every project. Databricks packaged that expertise into a ready-to-use platform and Solution Accelerators, so customers get expert-level results without hiring the experts.

From deterministic to probabilistic. Here are the new ways we build and use AI Products as Product Managers.

Loops & layers

Projects run in cycles. Self-healing products run in loops and layers, where each layer's output is the next one's input:

1Sense

What users do, who they are, what's changing in the macro space.

2Synthesise

Signals from layer one are cross-referenced against business goals and a fresh tech-business hypothesis is printed out.

3Ship

The smallest slice that moves the KPI is automatically built by agents with Human in the loop.

4Validate

Synthetic audience, previews and release rings make sure our rollout plan scales as we hit specific KPIs.

↻ With each loop our product becomes smarter, and iterations get cheaper and faster as we move towards a self-healing product.

Speed

No one is holding their breath for large release cycles, as they get replaced by a shorter cadence of slice deployments.

Ship→Measure→Repeat

You will know you're at the right pace when your release notes start looking like a chat thread.

Spotify

Spotify now ships about 4,500 production deployments a day. Getting from idea to production used to take weeks or months and now takes about an hour.

Building ahead of time is normal

Classical roadmaps commit only to what today's technology can reliably deliver. AI Products deliberately ship a capability before the model is fully ready to support it. For the first time in history you are literally building for tomorrow.

Anthropic

Anthropic built the harness for its code review feature before the model was good enough. The feature shipped as soon as model accuracy crossed the trust threshold the team had set. The team keeps a shelf of such prototypes and re-tests them with every model release.

AI way, compoundsships once

Compounding products

Traditional products treated each feature as a bespoke output that ships once and gets used many times.

With AI principles, the compounding value of solutions gets unleashed: the AI way makes your solutions smarter (and cheaper) over time and extends their application across multiple different cases (personas, industries etc.) in a more seamless manner.

owned and maintainedleft alone, decays

Dynamic products

Traditional tools are built once and stay roughly as capable as the day they shipped. An AI solution decays without active ownership and maintenance of its guardrails and eval context.

The classical team is organized by function: product, design, engineering, quality.

Product consistency will get jeopardized

Although each slice is deployed successfully and with a purpose, fast deployment in slices will leave your customer experience fragmented, adding friction and breaking the user's mental model. Continuous usability testing of the whole journey and a great partnership with the QA crew keep it feeling like one product.

You have completely new adoption frictions for your solutions

Identity threat

Expert users resist AI tools that imply "the machine knows better." Design AI in your solutions to support their judgment, not replace it.

Automation bias

Users who trust the AI too much stop checking it. Be transparent with confidence levels and sources, and send uncertain cases to a human.

Accountability gaps

AI solutions are still tools. If no one knows who owns an AI-assisted decision, its adoption might drop. Put a named person behind every decision.

Your role is changing

The execution part of your job is getting automated: backlog grooming, PRD drafting, ticket writing, status updates.

What stays is judgment, taste and vision: choosing the right problems to solve, defining what "good" looks like, looking ahead on the business and model capability, and designing the guardrails and eval sets.

04Judgment. Taste. Vision.

Uniquely human.

Models are always trained on what was already done. They have no access to what is felt, or to the shared experience behind genuine connection, as these topics don't have a ground truth for them to work from. This is why, when every operational aspect of your work as a Product Manager gets automated, your judgment, vision and taste are left. This is your authentic human perspective on work.

How do I know what to hand over to AI?

There are three flavours to choose from, and here is the cheat sheet below:

AutomationAugmentationAgentic

Automation

AI takes over the entire process with zero supervision.

The process is repetitive, has clear steps you can write in advance, it's tedious, not creative and always has the same goal.

Every run takes the same path of steps, and "good" output can simply be defined and clearly measured.

Errors are cheap and easy to spot.

Examples
  • Pulling name, date and total from an invoice.
  • Turning a list of specs into a product description.
  • Localising documentation while keeping its tone.

Augmentation

Human and AI work together. AI handles the volume while the human handles the judgment.

A good workflow candidate for Augmentation:

Has high volume and is pattern-rich, but the stakes are high, so accountability stays with the expert. This expert verifies the AI's output on quality, viability and compliance.

"Good" is judged by eye, not by a test. There is no objective pass or fail, so AI acts as a sparring partner surfacing outputs while the human sets the direction for what's "good".

Examples
  • AI drafts the requirements and design spec from the PM's intent, applying brand, security and UX rules as it writes. The product owner reviews the spec instead of writing it, as augmentation moves the human from creator to curator.
  • AI builds three throwaway prototypes of a new screen, so the team clicks through them and picks a direction by eye before any real code is written.

Agentic solutions

AI acts on its own toward a goal and decides alone on the next step at runtime.

It needs boundaries, but it doesn't follow a step-by-step script, because the right sequence of steps could change from run to run. A process is a good candidate for an agentic solution when:

The goal is clear, but the path isn't. The work has to be broken into subtasks and progress evaluated along the way, not at the end, so the plan gets adjusted as it goes.A tell-tale sign you need this workflow: you notice your automated workflow needed many exceptions.

It spans many sources across multiple data formats and needs to recall its memory to do its work.

Examples
  • Monitoring an industry news feed and alerting legal only when a regulatory change affects current products.
  • Extracting liability clauses from 50 contracts and flagging the ones that deviate from the standard.
A spectrum

Of course this is a spectrum: the same workflow moves from augmentation toward automation as trust and specification improve. Also, with each model release the line moves between these flows, so do revisit your decisions here.

Check your surroundings. Are you striking the right balance between your (human) work and AI?

Production reality

The critical datasource to help you navigate this complex question is Production reality.

Production reality exists long before your solution does. It represents how exactly the work is done today: the real people, the real workflows, the real data, the tools they already juggle and the workarounds they built to get through the day.

Your task as a Product Manager is to become familiar with it on two levels. Because your product will not land in empty space. It lands inside a rich context of actual workflows, and the psychological expectations and motivations of your stakeholders. Understanding both marks the difference between the audience accepting or rejecting your solution.

Ask yourself

Take a look around you, the solutions you build, the work you do. And answer for yourself:

  • How informed are you about the rich context of actual workflows and psychological expectations your audience has?
  • Would learning more about one or both help you make simpler and more effective solutions which your audience loves?
  • What is currently being done "because we've always done it this way" by yourself, your colleagues or your audience, which could clearly be improved?

Roadmap

A Roadmap is a hypothesised sequence of functionalities or solutions at a given moment, which is estimated to bring the biggest ROI in the shortest amount of time.

By its nature it is a snapshot in time. It is a dynamic artefact, and you own it. It changes as you develop and discover.

There's no tech roadmap and product roadmap, no person X and person Y roadmap (if they work in the same team), no tech-debt and regular development roadmap. There is one unified product roadmap.

It can carry infrastructure, features, design and process work. Not all of it touches users, but all of it creates value for the product.

Roadmap milestones try to focus on outcomes.

A roadmap item"Cut onboarding to one day"
A task"Build an onboarding experience"

Shorter horizon

In times of AI you should get used to deploying more frequently, as no one is holding their breath for large release cycles. These get replaced by a shorter cadence of slice deployments.

A 6–12 month roadmap sequence can be outdated before it's finished, because new model capabilities may double in the meantime. To manage this, plan in short cycles and revisit the roadmap with every major model release.

Traditional delivery has a roadmap and ships in large, infrequent release cycles. The AI way works in slices: the smallest production-ready increment that moves a target metric, deployed at a fast pace.

Claude Code

Anthropic's Claude Code team's six-month roadmap held up for about three months. They didn't respond with better forecasting; they simply started planning in shorter intervals.

Asana

Asana's product team uses an internal AI agent that knows the roadmap, the strategy and the reasons behind past trade-offs. When Marketing wants product input, they give it a task and it answers from the current roadmap knowledge base.

New shapes

Depending on who you're speaking with, there is more than one way to represent a roadmap.

Shape 1

Feature roadmap

What gets built and roughly when. Still the right shape for regulatory or compliance deadlines, mature and predictable solutions, and low-risk work on known tech.

Shape 2

Outcome roadmap

Instead of a list of features, each team gets a business objective and decides how to reach it. This is much better adjusted to the AI space.

Marty Cagan's (SVPG) example: onboarding a new customer takes 30 days. The roadmap item becomes "dramatically reduce the time for a new customer to go live", and success is measured as average onboarding under 3 hours.
Shape 3

Strategic intent

For leadership and non-technical stakeholders. No features and no dates: where we want to be, why it matters and which constraints guide us. In this format, the team keeps the freedom to choose the path.

"By Q4, cut median support ticket resolution time by 50% for mid-market customers, without adding headcount." Key bets: smarter ticket routing, auto-replies for simple cases, predictive escalation. Constraints: GDPR/SOC2, and CSAT stays at 85% or higher. If a bet doesn't work, the team drops it without asking for new approval.

The new risk

This way of working is becoming common and introduces a new risk in your work as a Product manager. A risk you will tackle by partnering with User Insights and Quality Assurance.

Continual usability testing and user behaviour monitoring are critical in catching whether a new slice introduces friction it shouldn't, breaks a user's mental model, fragments the experience across cohorts, or simply moves the problem instead of solving it.

You want to avoid deploying 100 individually successful slices which, in totality, break your product experience.

ROI & cost

In managing your AI Products, the marginal cost of each new use case, decision and customer onboarding should keep falling as you accumulate reusable IP (eval sets, prompt and agent patterns, retrieval indexes, cached context).

That said, your AI's cost-per-decision should be tracked as a first-class metric alongside KPI lift.

This means a deployed slice which moves a product KPI but blows its budget should be iterated on or sunset.

Roll-out

As AI models get updated and customers bring inputs you never tested on, your product can change after launch without anyone touching a line of code.

A rollout is a continuous process of releasing your product to your audience, in which each stage is unlocked via measured criteria.

Here is a standard checklist for releasing AI solutions.

1

Written conditions for roll-out

Each functionality you deploy is competing for its spot on production.

Have in writing what it needs to achieve in each deployment phase to progress to the next one.
Rollout example
0
Shadow mode

The AI runs on real production cases next to the current process, and no end-user sees its output.

Next phase is unlocked when the AI matches the current process on 99% of cases.
1
Friendly customers

3–5 existing end users who agree to try a beta version and give feedback in a structured manner you set up.

Next phase is unlocked when these users naturally switch over to the new solution in the majority of cases without being asked, and no critical issue is open.
2
One segment

5% of accounts in one group of similar customers (segment by usage type, pricing tier, geography etc.).

Next phase is unlocked when task success is at least 5% higher than in the group without your solution, and the cost per decision of your AI is within budget.
3
All segments

25% of accounts across every user group.

Next phase is unlocked when the same lift holds in each group individually, not only on average.
4
Everyone

All accounts. The old path is switched off and everyone uses the new solution.

If a solution misses its condition for a specific phase within its dedicated time, you can stop the progression to the following phase and iterate, or roll back with a feature flag (depending on the severity of the miss).

These conditions are to be shared with stakeholders before the rollout, so they are clear on any promotion or rollback activity.

Stripe

Stripe deploys continuously and runs 5.5 nines on its core APIs. Each change goes to a small number of machines first, then 1% of traffic, then a progressive rollout, so no single release carries the big risk.

2

Map the mental models waiting in production

Your product lands on expectations customers already hold, and mental frameworks developed from previous solutions.

Before launch, list the ones it will meet on day one, and plan the development and rollout around them as a way to manage expectations.

Anthropic reports first-attempt success of about one third for its coding agent, so it sets the expectation up front: try one-shot, then switch to collaborating.

Notion saw 18,000 agents created in the three weeks after launching External Agents, with 90% of usage running on automated triggers rather than chat. Customers used agents as standing processes, not as chatbots. Usage data, not the launch plan, showed how the product was actually adopted.

3

Let the cost of an error set the pace

The actual dynamic and guardrails of a solution's progression through roll-out phases depend on two things: how expensive an error is, and how quickly you would spot it.

Cheap and visible

The solution writes a draft for the user to edit before sending. You can take bigger steps in your rollout plan.

5%→50%of accounts · about a week per phase
Costly, hidden or more complex

Start in parallel with the existing process, with human approval on every action.

1%→5%→20%about a month per phase

Do mind, when running the new and old process in parallel: the old process still makes mistakes too, so compare against its measured error rate as well, not just against the correct rate.

4

Design adoption, then measure it per segment

Adoption is designed.

If possible, try following these rules to convey a frictionless experience of your AI solution:

  • Put the entry point inside the workflow segment where the work actually happens, not in a separate AI tab. You want to minimize product surfaces and cognitive load.
  • Usage unlocks usage. Figure out a way to track actions as kinetic energy which spreads and drives more usage. Statistics is your friend here (key concepts) in understanding how user actions are connected.
7 friendswithin the first10 days

Facebook famously calculated that if a newly registered user added 7 friends within their first 10 days, they reached the "Aha! moment." Beyond that threshold, their likelihood of becoming an active, long-term user skyrocketed. If they failed to reach that milestone, they almost always churned.

5

Measure the outcome your customer bought

Logins show the product is open, not that it works. Pick the one number that proves the customer got the result (a resolved ticket, a completed task) and report it next to usage.

$0.99per confirmed resolution

Intercom prices its Fin agent at $0.99 per confirmed resolution, so the outcome metric is also used as a clear way to invoice customers and show value.

Pricing

Technology serves the business.

The biggest marginal business lever for growth is not acquiring new customers, but pricing.

Price is an exchange rate

Pricing is the exchange rate between the value your customer receives (and perceives) and what they pay for it. This is the reason why you should get close to understanding how your end users interpret value from your product.

The gap between what customers would pay and what you charge is your revenue left on the table.

ProfitWell

Patrick Campbell's (ProfitWell) pricing data shows most companies underprice. "Finger in the air" pricing almost always means you're underselling.

Find your value metric

A value metric is the unit you charge for: seats, messages, rows synced, resolved tickets, API calls. A good value metric has three attributes:

It is easy to understandA customer should understand what they'll pay without a calculator. It grows as your customer succeeds It feels fair to your customers

There are four common ways to build a pricing strategy around your value metric:

Feature tiers

Pay more for advanced features.

Adobe: Photoshop only vs. the full suite

Light features + value metric

Few differences between plans; the price follows usage.

Slack, per user

Hybrid

Feature tiers plus a value metric.

Salesforce: plans priced per user

Pure value metric

The price follows usage only.

Twilio: per message or API call

Research, don't guess

Don't assume what customers value and how much they value it. There are simple willingness-to-pay exercises you can use to learn from your customers.

Early

Van Westendorp, when you need a broad price range.

Clear

Gabor-Granger, once the offer is clear. It asks about purchase intent at set price points, to find the price that realistically brings the most revenue.

Different customer segments see value differently. This fact is something you can use in pricing to maximize consumer surplus.

For example: features only some customers want (a lot) work best as add-ons.

The classic add-on

Priority support is the classic add-on. Patrick Campbell's data shows 20–30% of a customer base will pay for it. Price it at around 10% of the core product, and since you already support your customers, it's almost all pure margin. The only change is the sequence in which the support team receives requests.

Pricing is a continual conversation

Review pricing every 6 months as the product improves, and run pricing experiments every quarter.

The logic is simple: once you've delivered new value, you can raise the price.

Keep it simple. A customer should understand what they'll pay without a calculator. When you re-price, back it with research and protect existing customers (grandfathering), so it reads as value and not as a grab.

Pricing in times of AI

The fundamental change came when, outside of classic infra cost, new AI products started producing inference cost just by being used.

This means that the old freemium model, in which users had unlimited access to the product and its functionalities, has to change.

The freemium today has to show enough "magic" for users to hit the aha moment, but the free tier can end up good enough to replace the paid one.

When setting this up, the question on the Product Manager's table is: "How do I decide what to gate behind a paywall?", as freemium works as a great acquisition engine.

Google AI started with one premium tier built around its smartest model. It didn't work: the free tier already felt "smarter than I am" to most users, so few upgraded, while the power users who did pay burned so much compute that the economics were "terrifying".

Here is what they did. Google AI's product lead puts three things behind the paywall instead:

Usage intensity

Tiers by volume. Midjourney gives every plan the same image quality: you pay for fast GPU hours, and once they run out, your images wait in a slower queue.

Outcomes

Free gives you the answer, paid does the work. Chrome auto browse, where the agent clicks through websites and finishes the task for you, is only in Google's higher tiers and is sold on the hours of manual work it saves.

The heaviest modalities

Text stays free; video, 3D and simulation are paid. Genie 3, Google's real-time interactive world model, is top tier only. Serving it to hundreds of millions of free users wasn't just bad business: there weren't enough chips to do it.

Monetization

You have a new cost line item in your product work.

It's the price of every decision your AI makes for your customer. Each one is paid in model calls, tokens, retrieval and tool runs, so it is important to track it per decision, not only per customer.

Pricing sets the number. Monetization is the mechanics behind it: how the bill is built, how it behaves month to month, and whether each customer still earns you money.

Dropbox

Dropbox saw its spend on AI agents rise about 28x in a year. Instead of cutting it, the team started tracking cost per pull request and per Jira ticket, to see whether the money was going to the right problems.

Cost to serve sets the floor

Your cost per decision sets the floor of your price. The dynamic nature of pricing is enhanced even further by the fact that the floor moves with every model you choose and every new model release.

Predictability is a feature

Metered pricing is harder to predict than a flat fee. When a bill surprises, customers don't just question the price, they stop trusting the product. Bill shock is a product problem, not a billing one.

Fivetran charges per monthly active row (MAR). Over 2025–26 it moved volume discounts from the account to each connection and started charging for deleted and history rows. A backfill or schema change could add millions of billable rows overnight, and customers who thought they had 1M active rows had 4M. Many bills rose 40–70%, some 2–4x, and competitors built their pitch around it.

The fix is a structure your customer can budget for:

  • a committed base they agree to upfront
  • overage on top, priced in advance
  • real-time usage visibility, so alerts arrive before the spend and not with the invoice
Lebara

Lebara eSIM customers got bill shock coming back from holidays. Instead of handling it in support, the team built proactive notifications: a usage alert at 80% and a one-tap top-up. Predictability became a feature, not a support process.

AI keeps the bill under control

The same AI that adds a cost line also helps you manage the bill:

  • forecast usage per cohort and flag anomalies before the invoice goes out
  • answer "why did my bill change?" with the actual root cause
  • replay a planned price change on real customer bills before you ship it, so you see who gets hit and by how much

Product KPIs for agentic solutions

They differ from classic ones, and are grouped below.

Quality & Trust

  • Eval coverage: % of real production scenarios represented in your eval / golden set
  • Eval pass rate as a merge / release gate
  • Hallucination ratio
  • Regeneration rate: how often a user hits "retry / regenerate"
  • Guardrail trigger / policy-violation hit rate by the autonomous solution
  • Trace coverage: % of decisions with a complete, auditable trace / log
  • Re-engagement after error rate

Autonomy & Reliability

  • Autonomy rate based on task complexityHow many of the core complex tasks are confidently covered by AI, vs surface-level activities.
  • Types of tasks automated (based on complexity)
  • Ratio of AI decisions overridden by a human
  • Model drift frequency

Value & Adoption

  • Depth / organic (unprompted) adoptionDepth-of-use progression
  • Goal completion rate
  • Time-to-Data / Time-to-Value
  • Flywheel coverage: ratio of quality outputs fed back to the system
  • Synthetic-audience accumulation rate

Unit Economics

  • Inference cost-per-decision, weighed against the value each call creates
  • Cache hit rate
